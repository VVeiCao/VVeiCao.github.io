How It Works

1The 4D control

The objects of the image are lifted to 3D proxies. The user gives each proxy one rigid transform per frame, and a camera path. Here the dog walks round the car and jumps in. The proxies only slide and turn: the dog never moves a leg.

2Render the rigid depth

The posed proxies are rendered over the background depth, frame by frame. The result is the rigid depth: the placement of every object is exact, but the motion is that of a statue.

3Follow the blur, then generate

Stage 1 lets the video model redraw the depth while it follows only the blur of the render, so the dog rears up, grips the handle and jumps. Stage 2 generates the video from this refined depth and the input image.

Abstract

Controlling a video world model with text keeps its motion natural but leaves the placement of objects and the camera ambiguous. Existing 4D control methods resolve this ambiguity with 3D trajectories for objects and the camera, but these trajectories are coarse, so the methods struggle with complex motion such as interactions between objects. To obtain both precise placement and natural motion, we introduce Follow-the-Blur, a training-free framework that combines the spatial control of 4D trajectories with the motion prior of a pretrained video world model. Our key insight is that the coarse trajectories given by 4D control carry only low-frequency information, whereas the high-frequency details of natural motion, such as bending limbs and bodies in contact, are already encoded in this motion prior. To harness this prior, we render the 4D control as a depth video and refine it by our proposed dual-band denoising. In the low band, the model follows the blur of the render, while in the high band it synthesizes fine details from its prior. A second stage then generates the final video from the refined depth, in which bodies move and interact naturally. Experiments on Interact4D, our new benchmark of interactions among people, animals, and objects, show that Follow-the-Blur follows the 4D control more closely and yields more natural interactions than trained 4D control methods.
